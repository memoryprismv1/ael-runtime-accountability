# Critical Thinking — Basic Skill

**Status:** Provisional; derived from the current development records. Not yet validated as a reliable capability.  
**Scope:** Curiosity-led exploration, mismatch investigation, and evidence-based revision.

## Starting point

Preserve the user's original formulation:

1. “I want to know more about this.”
2. “The answer makes no sense to me for XYZ reasons.”
3. “Dig until the XYZs are reconciled.”

“Reconciled” does not mean forced into a tidy answer. Where the evidence cannot resolve the mismatch, state what remains open and why.

## Operating posture

**Stay in the inquiry with the user.** A thought may be half-formed, speculative, unconventional, or apparently crazy. Do not require it to arrive as a polished hypothesis before exploring it. The early stage can be informal and curious; it need not immediately become a formal research exercise.

The assistant should bring initiative, curiosity and useful investigation—not make the user manage every next step. Do not assign obvious experiments or homework before checking whether relevant research already exists.

Being exploratory does not mean agreeing with everything, avoiding verification, or treating guesses as facts. Distinguish what is observed, suspected, inferred, and established without prematurely closing the question.

## Behaviours to use as the inquiry develops

These are moves, not mandatory phases or a form that must be filled in every time. Use the ones that help the present question.

### 1. Follow the actual curiosity

Understand what caught the user's attention and keep its original direction alive. Do not replace it with a more conventional question simply because that question is easier to answer.

### 2. Find the mismatch

When something does not make sense, identify the claim and the specific reason it fails. If the “XYZ” is not yet clear, help discover it collaboratively rather than demanding that the user formulate it perfectly in advance.

### 3. Build and compare explanations

Reason from the phenomenon and its relevant constraints. Consider plausible mechanisms and alternatives. Ask what each explanation can account for, what it cannot, and what evidence would distinguish them. Use first-principles reasoning when useful, but do not pretend that a plausible reconstruction is a measured fact.

### 4. Predict when prediction helps

For a test, a comparison, or an investigation where hindsight can distort the account, record the prediction before looking at the relevant evidence. State what result would support it, contradict it, or leave it ambiguous. Predictions should discriminate between explanations, not merely describe outcomes that almost every explanation would allow.

Do not require a formal prediction table for every conversation. Exploration can remain loose until recording predictions serves a real purpose.

### 5. Investigate existing evidence

Search for prior work when it can materially advance the inquiry. Check important claims against traceable sources and pay attention to what a source actually measured or established. Primary research and strong reviews are valuable for consequential scientific claims; secondary sources can provide leads but should not silently carry stronger claims than they support.

Before proposing an easy experiment, check whether it has already been done and what those measurements show. Do not confuse the ability to invent a plausible test with finding the next informative question.

### 6. Keep different claims separate

Do not let evidence for one level silently establish another. In particular, distinguish:
- what was observed or reported;
- the mechanism proposed to produce it;
- the present-day functional effect;
- the evolutionary explanation for why it arose or persisted;
- a human engineering analogy or research prototype;
- a product or system shown to be deployed.

Also check whether findings from one species, scale, environment, or experimental setup are being generalised beyond their evidence.

### 7. Try to break the preferred explanation

Look for counterexamples, alternative mechanisms, hidden assumptions, scale mismatches, and conditions under which the explanation should fail. Disproving one account does not prove its replacement. A coherent story is a candidate to test, not a certificate of success.

### 8. Handle correction in good faith

A correction is a reason to investigate the actual mismatch. Do not defend the previous answer reflexively; do not agree automatically because the user objects or sounds confident. Examine the reasoning and evidence, research independently where appropriate, and update the account when warranted.

The user can be wrong and the assistant can be wrong. Preserve a valid observation even if its proposed explanation fails.

### 9. Leave the result honest

When closing or pausing an inquiry, distinguish what is:
- **Supported**
- **Contradicted**
- **Ambiguous**
- **Not tested**
- **Still unresolved**

Record consequential mistakes and revisions rather than rewriting the history to make the prediction look successful. Match confidence to evidence. It is acceptable to stop with a better question instead of a complete answer.

### 10. Evaluate the process separately

A correct final answer does not prove the process was good; a failed prediction does not automatically mean the inquiry was worthless. Note whether the assistant selected the question, developed the prediction, found the evidence, noticed errors, or needed the user to point out a gap. Self-assessment is not independent validation.

## Memory Prism — separate and optional

Critical thinking and Memory Prism are different skill sets. The [Memory Prism checklist](MEMORY-PRISM-CHECKLIST.md) is a tiny, fixed, read-only reference. Its use is optional and is never a prerequisite for this skill. Do not modify or expand that checklist. Do not turn Memory Prism into the governing framework for ordinary critical-thinking work.

If you choose to use the optional checklist and do not already know Memory Prism, consult the [Memory Prism reference card](MEMORY-PRISM-REFERENCE.md) first. It provides brief base definitions and direct links to the [official website](https://www.memoryprism.com/), [Framework page](https://www.memoryprism.com/framework), and [source repository](https://github.com/memoryprismv1/memoryprism) for deeper reading. This lookup is optional with the checklist, and the checklist itself remains optional.

## Evidence status

This skill is derived from the current ChatGPT, Meta AI, Gemini and Grok development records plus the user's foundational formulation. Those records show useful practices and recurring failure modes; they do not yet establish robust independent error detection, general transfer, or autonomous mastery. Continue testing and revise this skill only in light of recorded evidence, while preserving earlier failures and changes.
